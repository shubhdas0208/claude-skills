# NAV (Net Asset Value)

**What it is:** The value of one unit of a fund, computed daily. **NAV = (Total assets − liabilities and accrued expenses) ÷ Units outstanding.** Pure arithmetic — pool value ÷ units — with no valuation content.

**How to read it:** A high NAV means an old fund and/or good compounding history — that's it. NAV is useful for two things: (1) computing holding value and gains (units × NAV), and (2) detecting a payout — a sharp NAV drop without a matching market fall probably means an IDCW payout. What grows your money is the *percentage change* in NAV, which depends only on fund holdings, not the starting level.

**Thresholds / zones:** None — NAV isn't that kind of number.

**The trap:** Reading NAV like a stock price — "₹22 is cheaper than ₹200, more room to grow." Wrong. A stock price relates to a claim on a business, so price-vs-value matters; NAV doesn't. Identical-portfolio funds behave identically regardless of NAV — you just get fewer units of the "expensive" one.

**Strong answer mentions:**
- NAV formula: (assets − liabilities/expenses) ÷ units outstanding
- No valuation content, unlike a stock price
- High NAV signals fund age/compounding history, not cheapness
- Identical-portfolio funds behave identically regardless of NAV level
- Legitimate uses: holding-value math, detecting IDCW payouts
