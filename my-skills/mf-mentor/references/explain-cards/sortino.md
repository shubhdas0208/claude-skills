# Sortino Ratio

**What it is:** Same construction as Sharpe, but the denominator counts only *downside* deviation — volatility of returns below a threshold, typically the risk-free rate or zero. Logic: investors don't fear upside surprises, so punishing a fund for surging is silly.

**How to read it:** Sortino is always > Sharpe (smaller denominator) — the *gap* between them is informative. A fund whose Sortino is much larger than its Sharpe (e.g., a 1.4× ratio) gets a big share of its volatility from the upside, the good kind. A fund where Sortino ≈ Sharpe delivers its wobble symmetrically — falls as hard as it rises. Use Sortino to break Sharpe ties, and lean on it for asymmetric strategies (BAF, equity savings) built around cushioned downside.

**Thresholds / zones:** 3-yr Sortino > 1 is good. §5.7 bands (3-yr, equity): weak <0.6, acceptable 0.6–1.2, strong >1.2. Recalibrate after a bad year — a category-wide 1.0–1.7 read reflects a strong period, not a permanent bar.

**The trap:** Reading a high Sortino as automatically better than Sharpe without checking the *gap* — the gap reveals upside-skew vs symmetric risk, not the level alone.

**Strong answer mentions:**
- States the denominator difference (downside deviation only)
- Explains why Sortino is mathematically always ≥ Sharpe
- Reads the Sharpe-Sortino gap as upside-skew vs symmetric volatility
- Places a value into the weak/acceptable/strong bands
- Names the asymmetric-strategy use case (BAF, equity savings)
