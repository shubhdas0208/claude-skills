# Treynor Ratio

**What it is:** Treynor = (Fund return − Risk-free rate) ÷ Beta — excess return per unit of *market* risk, ignoring fund-specific wobble. Theory: in a diversified portfolio of funds, fund-specific noise diversifies away, so only market sensitivity deserves compensation.

**How to read it:** Use it when comparing well-diversified funds within a category, or funds as components of a portfolio. Unlike Sharpe (a pure ratio), Treynor is in %-points — rank funds with it, don't interpret absolute levels as good/bad. For concentrated/focused or low-R² funds (beta itself unreliable), Treynor misleads; Sharpe is the safer general tool. When Sharpe and Treynor disagree — fund A beats B on Treynor but loses on Sharpe — A carries more non-market (concentration) risk; that's your cue to check the portfolio.

**Thresholds / zones:** None given — rank with Treynor, don't read absolute levels (illustrative screener range was 4.9–20.8, not a good/bad scale).

**The trap:** Applying Treynor to a concentrated or low-R² fund, where an unreliable beta makes the ratio meaningless — or reading its raw number as pass/fail instead of a ranking tool.

**Strong answer mentions:**
- States the formula: beta (market risk only) is the denominator
- Identifies the right use case: diversified funds, or funds as portfolio components
- Flags Treynor as unreliable for concentrated/low-R² funds
- Reads a Sharpe-vs-Treynor disagreement as non-market (concentration) risk
- Treats the number as a ranking tool, not an absolute-level score
